Running on Empty

The buildup to Bangladesh's energy crisis

Bangladesh's energy crisis did not begin with the recent gas shortages. It has been years in the making, as domestic gas production failed to keep pace with rapidly rising demand from power plants, industries and households. Despite massive investments in electricity generation that significantly expanded installed capacity, fuel supply gradually emerged as the sector's biggest vulnerability. As mature gas fields depleted and major new discoveries remained elusive, the country increasingly turned to imported fuels to keep factories and power plants running.

Yet despite the growing dependence on imported fuel, the system remained fragile. All imported LNG enters the national grid through just two floating storage and regasification units (FSRUs) off Moheshkhali, with little redundancy to absorb disruptions.

Then there is an even deeper layer of risk: Bangladesh's growing exposure to geopolitical shocks. The conflict involving Iran and the subsequent closure of the Strait of Hormuz, a critical artery for global LNG trade, left Bangladesh scrambling for supply. Qatar, Bangladesh's largest LNG supplier, cut contracted deliveries by half, while spot market prices surged amid tightening global competition.

As the country scrambled to secure replacement cargoes at elevated prices, a fire at the Excelerate Energy terminal on July 21 abruptly knocked out a major import route, wiping out an estimated 17 percent of the nation's gas supply overnight. The result is a crisis that extends far beyond energy.

Bangladesh reportedly has atleast 29 gas fields and one oil field, with reserves as of February 2025. Twenty of the gas fields were still producing. The Global Shockwave and the Domestic Trap

Two things culminated into the energy crisis in Bangladesh in 2026. One — a geopolitical shock — was sudden and foreign. The other was domestic and had been arriving slowly for seven years.

The route

Ras Laffan to Moheshkhali is about 5,600 km of sea, and every kilometre of it depends on one 39 km wide strait.

Chokepoint
Spot cargo origin
The channel On 28 February the US and Israel went to war with Iran. Iran took hold of the Strait of Hormuz, the channel that carries about one fifth of the world's LNG. Bangladesh buys most of its gas through that channel.
The break Iran declared the strait closed on 18 April, hours after opening it. 20 to 23 percent of Bangladesh's imported fuel oil transits Hormuz. 100 percent of its imported crude does.
Where the gas came from instead Nineteen cargoes arrived from Qatar before the war. Thirty five spot cargoes have been bought since March, from wherever they could be found.

The spot market is not a safety net. Spot market prices are highly volatile, have no upper ceiling, and are impacted by global bidding wars. Developing nations like Bangladesh must outbid wealthier economies to secure cargo. While normal baseline prices range from $10 to $12 per MMBtu, geopolitical shocks can make the prices skyrocket. In 2022, after Russia invaded Ukraine, spot LNG reached about $60. To compare, the August 2026 cargo cost $21.55. That is the warning, not the relief.

The valve closes

The terminal shut down. Regasification stopped. Four hundred and fifty million cubic feet a day left the system.

The number

That was 17 percent of all the gas in the country. Half the LNG supply.

National supply fell from 2,620 to 2,170 million cubic feet a day.

Fifty seven percent

The country met 57 percent of demand.

The gap is 1,630 MMcfd, derived by subtracting supply from the 3,800 MMcfd demand figure.

The grid takes the hit

Power plants had been getting about 900 million cubic feet a day. That dropped to 650 million cubic feet per day. Gas generation fell from 5,200 megawatts to 3,500. The Power Development Board started burning oil instead.

What it cost

Gas power cost about Tk 31 crore a day. Oil power cost Tk 90 crore and produced less than half as much.

Per megawatt generated, furnace oil ran roughly six times the cost of gas. Every hour the terminal stayed down, the state bought the same electricity six times over.

A two-node import system has no redundancy by construction. Losing one node removes about half the imported supply, every time, whatever the cause. Cyclone, maintenance, or fire, the failure mode is identical. This is a design flaw, not a run of bad luck.

The Economic Pressure Cooker

Below the pressure a process needs, output does not fall. It stops.

A ceramic kiln needs 30 to 35 pounds per square inch (psi). A knitwear dyeing line needs at least 10 psi. A CNG pump needs 7 or 8 psi. In late July almost nobody got what they needed. Every row is on the same scale: the bar is what arrived, the red mark is what the process requires.

Where a figure was reported as a range, the bar runs to the top of the range. Industry associations and company officials

Pressure failures are not proportional. A dyeing line running at a fifth of the pressure it needs does not produce a fifth of its normal output. It produces ruined fabric. Ceramics puts it plainly: yield falls from 90 percent to 10.

Nine price changes in four months, and cooking gas moved both ways

Between April and August the state changed the price of energy nine times. Fuel went up twice. Electricity went up once and was partly walked back the next day. Cooking gas went up, up, down, down, then up.

Energy and Mineral Resources Division and BERC orders

Fuel prices rose on 18 April even as Brent fell 9 percent to $90.

The April 18 hike is the tell. Crude was falling. The government raised prices anyway, mid-month, breaking its own monthly adjustment rule. It was the first mid-month change since the automatic mechanism began in 2024. The trigger was not the world price. It was the accumulated loss on the state's own books, and the point at which it could no longer be carried.

The Human Toll

Load shedding is a scheduling word. What it means is a five year old who cannot sleep.

The dark was not shared evenly

Daily load shedding by grid zone, in megawatts. Through August the national shortfall climbed past 3,000 MW, and no two zones went dark the same way.

Dhaka, Gandaria

In Dhaka, piped gas ran out before ten in the morning. Families cooked on rice cookers and paid for gas and electricity both.

Batiaghata, Khulna

In Batiaghata, more than 500 shrimp farms watched their aerators stop. When the aerators stop, oxygen falls and the shrimp begin to die. Small farmers cannot afford a generator for hours a day.

Cumilla

In Cumilla, draining a fish pond takes one night. It took three.

Kaliakair, Gazipur

In Kaliakair, residents surrounded the electricity office and blocked the Dhaka to Tangail highway. They went home after being promised improvement.

The regulator is allowed 60 days to decide. It took 30. Asked about the speed, the chairman said the decision was made quickly with the national budget in mind.

The rollback that moved the cost

A day later the regulator withdrew the increase for 1.62 crore lifeline consumers. It also noted that the lost revenue would have to be covered by additional government subsidy.

The rollback did not remove the cost. It returned it to the subsidy account the tariff rise was meant to relieve. Even after the increase, the power board still needs about Tk 41,000 crore a year.

More import capacity is therefore more subsidy liability. Imported LNG costs about Tk 65 a cubic metre and sells to industry at Tk 30. Unless tariffs roughly double, every cubic metre of new capacity enlarges the fiscal hole it was built to fix.

The stated rationale is that private imports reduce the subsidy burden. That does not follow—the subsidy exists because fuel sells below import cost. A private importer held to the same regulated price needs the same support. One free to set its own price shifts the burden to consumers. Either way the cost does not vanish — it moves.

The counter-argument

A single state buyer is a single point of failure in procurement, exactly as a single terminal is in supply. Competition could improve resilience even where it does not cut cost.

The cargo that was turned away

A Saudi Aramco shipment was due on Wednesday aboard the Al Hamra, a Moss type carrier built in 1997 that holds about 1.35 lakh cubic metres. Neither terminal accepted the vessel. The technical and insurance requirements it failed to meet have not been disclosed, and its age and containment system do not by themselves establish the reason. Asked why the problem had not surfaced earlier, a Petrobangla official said the supplier normally selects the tanker. Petrobangla has asked Aramco for a replacement ship, which the purchase agreement allows.

We had nothing to do with that. We tried to negotiate, but as there was a risk of another disruption, the terminal authorities did not accept it.
